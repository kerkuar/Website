When I was a boy on the coast between Fier and Vlora, among dusty roads that smelled of summer, I learned to recognise wealth by the way it came down from the sky. It arrived on helicopters that were not ours, painted with a flag I knew only from television. It was 1991, or perhaps 1992; for a child those years have no dates, only colours and sounds. I remember the adults’ confused voices, the waiting that stretched across endless summer days, the word “aid” repeated like a prayer, and a promise no one could explain and everyone felt: that across the sea, beyond the Adriatic, there was a place where things simply existed, and that soon they would reach us too.

That place had a name: Italy. For us the West was not an idea but an image, and the image was Italian television, which since the early 1980s had reached Albanian homes from a relay in Montenegro, against the regime’s will, in what Albanians still remember as the [“war of the antennas.”](<https://www.eastjournal.net/archives/75501>) The sea was the only membrane between us and a paradise watched on a delay; and when the dam of communism broke, the operation by which Italy came to feed us bore the name of a bird: [Pelican](<https://www.anae.it/italfor-pellicano/>). September 1991, a thousand Italian soldiers with helicopters and patrol boats, ninety thousand tonnes of flour and medicine unloaded at the ports of Durrës and Vlora: a bird that, in Christian legend, tears open its own breast to feed its young.

Thirty-five years later another bird gives its name to a revolt, and this one brings nothing to Albania: it is what the revolt defends. It is the flamingo of the Narta lagoon, which in the summer of 2026 became the emblem of the [largest Albanian mobilisation](<https://www.rosalux.de/en/news/id/54893/understanding-albanias-flamingo-revolution>) since the fall of communism. Between the two birds, the pelican that brings and the flamingo that resists, lies the whole story of Albania’s relationship with the West it once dreamed of; and there is a fact that few, outside the Balkans, have noticed: the ships did, in the end, arrive. Only the cargo was not for us.

## What is for sale

The ships arrived in the form of a resort. They let Ivanka Trump and Jared Kushner “discover” an island and decide to build private luxury resorts on it. Off the coast of Vlora lies Sazan, the largest Albanian island, for half a century a sealed military zone and, for that very reason, left untouched; opposite it, on the mainland, the Narta lagoon and the beach of Pishë-Poro, inside a protected area that is home to two or three hundred bird species. On that double site, the island and the lagoon, a vast luxury tourist complex is planned, tied to the Trumps and made possible by two decisions taken far from public view: a 2015 law that grants fast-track privileges to “strategic investors,” and a 2024 amendment that weakened environmental protections precisely where they were needed.

The detail that lit the fuse was a fence. At the end of May 2026 the bulldozers cut roads into the sand and barbed wire ringed the lagoon; and that fence, the urban planner Doriana Musai observes, [“makes a relation of power physically visible,”](<https://www.balcanicaucaso.org/en/cp_article/the-flamingo-revolution-for-a-different-albania/>) because it separates those allowed in from those shut out, so that pulling it down became a political act. From there the protest grew and changed in nature, and within weeks its slogan moved from “Albania is not for sale” to “Rama to prison, Berisha to prison,” and on to a demand for a clean slate: “New Albania.” The crowd rejected every party wholesale, not only Prime Minister Edi Rama, in power for thirteen years, but also his eternal rival Sali Berisha, who had defended the resort.

It even rejected its own possible faces. When the country’s most beloved singer, Alban Skënderaj, took the protesters’ side, he did so by [writing](<https://www.panorama.com.al/kengetari-i-famshem-alban-skenderaj-mbeshtet-protesten-e-drejta-duhet-degjuar-jam-ne-mbeshtetje-te-transparences/>) that the movement needs no famous name to be legitimate, “because you are the stage, you are the protagonists”; and that its victory, already won, is that “ordinary people have found one another” and discovered they are neither alone nor a minority. It is the closest thing to a manifesto this leaderless revolt has allowed itself.

## The promise of 1991

How could a lagoon set a whole country alight? To answer, one has to go back to that flag on the helicopters, and recall that for thirty years Albania was perhaps the most pro-Western country in Europe, and with good reason. The scholar [Enis Sulstarova](<https://zdjp.si/wp-content/uploads/2015/10/ASHS_22-2012-2_Sulstarova.pdf>) has surveyed the way Albanian intellectuals have told their own story, and found in it a recurring figure: national history as a pendulum between East and West, each era good if it carried the country towards the West and bad if it dragged it back East. It is a long flight from the Orient, in which the Ottoman, the communist, and even the Muslim (in a majority-Muslim country) become by turns the “other” to be kept at arm’s length. It was no accident that the first slogan of the protests against the dictatorship, in December 1990, was, as the novelist Ismail Kadare recalls, “We want Albania to be like Europe,” shouted days before the statue of Enver Hoxha was dragged through the streets.

There is something in that longing that the philosopher [Arlind Qori](<https://www.academia.edu/120626412/The_Albanian_Limbo_From_Bureaucratic_Socialism_to_Neoliberal_Capitalism_Arlind_Qori_and_Sofokli_Meksi_1_>), now one of the voices in the square, has looked at closely: Albanians loved Europe, he writes, the way Engels’s poor loved heaven, because, stripped of everything on earth, they imagined a kingdom of plenty somewhere else. And that heaven, for them, had a very precise address, which one of his own childhood memories captures better than any treatise: in the early 1990s a rumour went round that, once the elections were won, an almost endless line of European ships, queued from the ports of Italy all the way to the island of Sazan and loaded with goods, stood ready to dock and hand out wealth to everyone. The West had a concrete shape: Italian ships, bound for Sazan itself.

## The Italian mirror

Here Italy stops being a backdrop and becomes a mirror, and it is worth holding its gaze, because Italy was not only Albania’s dream; it was once [its master](<https://balkaninsight.com/2023/07/17/italy-still-views-albania-through-a-colonial-lens/bi/>). On 7 April 1939, twenty-two thousand Italian soldiers and four hundred aircraft occupied the country in a single day; King Zog fled, and Albania became a Fascist protectorate until 1943, with Albanians in a subordinate place and a racial hierarchy dressed up as generosity: “we are helping them improve,” the propaganda said.

Of all this, in Italy, almost no memory remains, and the historian Angelo Del Boca said so with a sharpness that deserves to be carved in stone: ***“the real difference between us and the other countries that had colonial empires is our persistent will to erase this past from collective memory.”*** The myth of the [italiani brava gente](<https://confronti.net/2023/02/colonialismo-italiano-una-giornata-della-memoria-contro-il-falso-mito-degli-italiani-brava-gente/>), the good-hearted Italians, has covered over the internment camps, the poison gas, the discrimination, and it endures. It matters here for a reason that is not historical etiquette, because the shape of that erasure, “we are good people, we only come to help,” is the same shape in which the handover of the coast presents itself today: no longer armies but capital, no longer a protectorate but “investment” and “friendship.” Domination has not disappeared; it has changed vehicles, and it still runs through Albania. The migrant centres that Giorgia Meloni’s Italy built at Gjadër, on Albanian soil, are only the latest time Rome has used that territory for a need of its own.

## The anatomy of a sell-off

It would be convenient, and false, to make Sazan merely the latest foreign incursion: the ground, for Kushner, was already prepared, and years of domestic choices prepared it. To follow them, it helps to lean on three Albanian scholars who, read together, describe the same mechanism from three sides.

The first side is language. [Blendi Kajsiu](<https://www.routledge.com/A-Discourse-Analysis-of-Corruption-Instituting-Neoliberalism-Against-Corruption/Kajsiu/p/book/9781472431301>) has studied the way Albania talks about “corruption,” and noticed a curious effect: all the talk of corruption ended up protecting the system rather than correcting it, because if every ill in the country is reduced to the “corruption” of some individual, then the very way the state was dismantled and sold off never comes to trial, and the sell-off of public wealth can present itself as a moral cleansing.

Arlind Qori, the philosopher of the square we have already met, takes the point a step further: the great “anti-crime” laws [strike the small fish and absolve the big ones](<https://lefteast.org/crime-or-ideology/>), so that “the system makes people believe that the cause of their misery lies not in the basic economic choices but in a handful of criminals to be expelled.” The anger, in other words, is funnelled towards the wrong target.

The third side is money, and here it is worth slowing down. The anthropologist [Smoki Musaraj](<https://www.cornellpress.cornell.edu/tales-from-albarado/>) has studied the financial pyramids that in 1996-97 swallowed the savings of two-thirds of the country and pushed it to the edge of civil war. I was there that New Year’s Eve, the night the neighbour emptied his magazine into the black sky while I crouched in the corridor in fear. She showed that this was not a collective “madness,” as the IMF liked to repeat, but an extreme and even logical form of the speculative economy then opening up to the country. That form did not die in 1997: it migrated, and today it lives in concrete, because the towers that have changed the skyline of Tirana, which the Albanian press now calls “the new pyramids,” and the resorts on the coast work in the same way, as a value that rises because everyone believes it will rise, until it collapses.

The three sides meet in a single investigation. In June 2026 the anti-corruption prosecutor’s office, [SPAK](<https://www.reporter.al/2026/06/21/raporti-konfidencial-protestat-te-shkaktuara-nga-pakenaqesi-legjitime/>), froze some 138 million euros linking the resort’s land at Zvërnec to the land beneath Tirana’s towers: money that, according to the case files, came from Latin American cocaine routes and was being laundered through those same two building sites, the resorts on the coast and the high-rises in the capital. A local economist put it without euphemism: if the government stopped issuing permits for the towers, the Albanian economy itself would struggle to stand. Construction is not one sector among others; it is the engine, and it runs on money whose origin no one truly knows.

There is one last document, and it is the most eloquent of all. While Rama was on television denouncing a “hybrid war” orchestrated by Greece, Iran, and Russia, his own government was circulating, in the embassies, a report it had commissioned from an American firm that analyses online campaigns; and that report, when read, contradicts the prime minister, for it concedes that the protest’s grievances are “legitimate” and sincere, and because out of 879,067 messages examined it isolates seven traceable to a Russian network. Seven. The content in Greek, the “proof” of the Greek hand waved about on television, is less than one per cent. The state had hired a foreign company to prop up its own version of events, and the company had to tell it it was wrong.

## The double movement

So much for the ways a country is sold; the truly new thing, the one that turns an environmental protest into a historic event, is the reaction, and to name it we need a book eighty years old. If Kajsiu and Musaraj explain how a country lets itself be sold, the economist [Karl Polanyi](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Great_Transformation_(book)>) explains why, at a certain point, it stops letting itself be sold. In his most famous book, ***The Great Transformation*** (1944), he makes an observation that sounds obvious and yet changes everything: land, labour, and nature are not commodities like the others, because no one produced them in order to sell them, and when the market insists on treating them as commodities anyway, society sooner or later reacts, almost by instinct, to pull them back out of exchange. To this double step, first everything is put up for sale, then society defends itself, Polanyi gives a name: the “double movement.”

The flamingo revolution is exactly this: a society moving to take back the land, the water, the coast, and lift them out of the auction, so that “Albania is not for sale” can be read as Polanyi reduced to a slogan. And here an old misunderstanding dissolves, because the freedom that arrived in 1991, the philosopher [Lea Ypi](<https://www.penguin.co.uk/books/441330/free-by-ypi-lea/9780241482742>) has written, was misread: it was handed over together with the brutal cure of privatisation, as if to vote and to sell off the state were the same wish. They were not. The square today is not asking for less market; it is asking that some things be kept out of the market. No one misses Hoxha. But people have discovered that freedom is not the licence to buy everything; it is the power of a community to defend its own future, beginning with water, the real wealth of a green country in a Mediterranean that is drying out.

## Not resentment, but a condition

One objection remains, and it is worth meeting head-on: is this not, at bottom, the familiar resentment of the East towards a West that humiliated it? Two scholars, [Ivan Krastev and Stephen Holmes](<https://www.foreignaffairs.com/reviews/capsule-review/2020-02-11/light-failed-why-west-losing-fight-democracy>), told the story of Eastern Europe’s last thirty years in just this way, as an “age of imitation” in which one had to become the West, and in which imitation, humiliating by its nature, bred the resentment from which Orbán’s populism grew. It is a powerful thesis.

And one that Albania reverses: here the flight West really does end, but without resentment, because in the square, at times, the American flags march beside the placards reading “Ivanka, go home.” This is not a rejection of the West; it is the first time Albanians have set conditions on it, telling the West of values apart from the West of buyers.

Nor is it an isolated case, but a Balkan script: in Serbia tens of thousands defend the [Jadar valley](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Serbian_environmental_protests>) against an EU-backed lithium mine that would erase twenty-two villages, while in Bosnia citizens tear down fences along the rivers. These are the revolts that certain anthropologists, studying the post-war Balkans, had in a sense foreseen: the revolt of a generation that stops looking for “the right man” to vote for and starts guarding what belongs to everyone.

I would be dishonest, though, if I let it be thought that the square holds only enemies of the market: many would want more investment, not less, and the most radical reading, the one that sees capital and imperialism everywhere, flattens a movement that is in fact very mixed; the nationalist strand, too, has its contradictions, and the antisemitic notes aimed at Kushner, for instance, must be named and refused. But the underlying demand is not for paralysis: it is for transparency, and for a few things to be kept out of the auction. It is something smaller than a revolution and larger than an election.

## The two wings

The pelican brought food, and with it the dream of the West as a cornucopia awaited in the fields and at the port; the flamingo brings nothing, lives in the lagoon, and is defended. Between the two wings everything has passed: a country that defined itself first by shutting the world out, then by letting it in without a single condition, and now, for the first time, by setting a price below which it will not sell.

I crossed that sea myself at eleven, in the opposite direction to the ships we dreamed of, towards Italy; and perhaps that is why today’s scene surprises me more than any other. The ships did, in the end, arrive at Sazan, and for the first time Albania stayed on the shore to say what it would not load aboard. This is not a farewell to the West. It is, if anything, the day a country discovers what the freedom it was promised actually means.
